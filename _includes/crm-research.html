Sources, units, and interpretation

A · How firms adjust. We trace cash flow and financing after material-price changes, comparing the component associated with identified supply-tightening events with the remaining price variation. For a predicted cost increase of 1% of identified revenue, the event-driven estimates at quarter 4 imply cumulative cash flow lower by 0.391% of initial assets and net external financing higher by 0.692%. This pattern is consistent with firms meeting liquidity needs through external finance. Shading and error bars show 95% confidence intervals; “Before” shows pre-event responses. The comparison series labeled “Predictable price changes” also includes pre-announcement price movements.

B · What past events imply for firms today. We apply historical event-driven price changes to firms’ 2024–2025 material exposures, using the estimated quarter-4 cash-flow and financing responses. Across all 28 replays, the implied amounts are $20.0 billion of cash flow at risk and $35.4 billion of external financing. These are scenarios, not recorded losses; totals include offsetting price declines. The brown segments show relationships where specialist reports miss uses accounting for more than half of a firm’s material dependence. Their size shows how relying on those reports alone can miss financially important exposure.

C · How dependence relates to equity risk. We compare firms within narrow industries and years, accounting for country conditions and standard firm characteristics. Moving from median to 90th-percentile dependence is associated with 1.79 percentage points higher annualized return volatility and 0.15 points higher expected shortfall—a measure of losses on the worst trading days. Holding dependence fixed, a one-standard-deviation increase in material-price risk is associated with 0.74 points higher return volatility. Both the extent of dependence and the volatility of the underlying materials help explain differences in equity risk. These are adjusted associations, not causal estimates.

Physical dependence connects material supply shocks to firms’ liquidity needs and equity risk. Event replays reveal the scale of financial exposure—including relationships absent from specialist reports.
